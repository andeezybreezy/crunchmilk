RV Campground Budget Calculator

Budget your RV campground costs for a trip

What This Calculator Actually Adds Up

RV campground costs aren't a single rate — they're three different markets. Private RV parks and resorts (KOA, Sun RV, independent parks) charge for full hookups, wifi, a pool, and often a rec room, and that convenience runs $45–75 a night in most of the country, climbing past $100 near national park gates or on the coast in summer. Public campgrounds — state parks, national parks, national forest campgrounds, and Corps of Engineers lakes — trade some or all hookups for a lower rate, typically $20–40 a night, often in a nicer setting. Free camping — BLM dispersed sites, national forest dispersed sites, Harvest Hosts, or a single night in a big-box parking lot — costs nothing but comes with no hookups and sometimes no cell signal. Enter how many nights fall into each bucket and this calculator multiplies private nights by $55 and public nights by $30 (national averages), leaving free nights at $0, to give you a total and a per-night average.

Where Membership Programs Fit In

The three-tier split above is the baseline; membership programs change the math within it without changing which bucket a night belongs to. Passport America roughly halves the rate at participating private parks, so a $60 site can drop to $30 — but the discount is usually capped to one or two nights per stay at each park and often blacked out during peak season, so read the restrictions before counting on it. Thousand Trails works differently: you pay an annual zone fee (commonly $500–700 for a single zone, more for multi-zone access) and most nights inside the system then cost $0, which only beats paying per-night if you're realistically logging 25–30+ nights a year at member parks. Escapees, Good Sam, and KOA Rewards give smaller 10–15% discounts that add up over a long trip but won't move you into a cheaper tier by themselves. None of these show up as a separate field in the calculator — instead, lower your assumed private-park rate manually if a membership covers most of your private nights, since the $55 default is an unmembered average.

A Worked Example: 14 Nights, Three Lodging Types

Say you're routing a two-week trip through the Southwest: 4 nights at private RV resorts near towns for laundry and full hookups, 6 nights at state parks along the route, 2 nights at Harvest Hosts wineries, and 2 nights boondocking on BLM land outside a national park. At national-average rates, the private nights run about 4 × $58 ≈ $232, the state park nights run about 6 × $32 ≈ $192, and the Harvest Hosts and BLM nights are $0 in camping fees, though it's worth budgeting $20–30 per Harvest Hosts stop for the purchase that's the unwritten cost of admission. That totals roughly $424 in campground fees for 14 nights — an average of just over $30 a night — versus about $770 if all 14 nights had been booked at private parks. Shifting two more nights from private to public or free would push the total under $370.

How It Works

A campground budget is really three different price tiers stacked together, not one number. Private RV parks and resorts with full hookups typically run $45–75 a night; public campgrounds — state parks, national parks, national forests, Corps of Engineers lakes — usually land in the $20–40 range with partial or no hookups; and free camping (BLM land, national forest dispersed sites, Harvest Hosts, a single night in a big-box lot) costs $0 but drops the hookups entirely. This calculator sorts your trip into those three buckets and multiplies each by a representative nightly rate so you get a total that reflects how you actually plan to camp, not a single flat average.

The basic rule:

  • Total Cost — Cost = (Private Nights × $55) + (Public Nights × $30) — Average rates for private and public campgrounds.

The $55 and $30 figures are national averages — a full-hookup site near a national park gate or on the coast in summer can run $80–120, while a no-frills gravel pad in rural farm country might be $25. The split between private, public, and free nights moves your total far more than the exact per-night figure does: shifting two or three nights from a private resort to a state park or a free BLM site can cut a two-week trip's campground bill by $150–300. Re-run the numbers once you have real reservations in hand, and swap in the actual quoted rates for anywhere you've already booked.

Tips & Considerations

  • Passport America cuts the rate roughly in half at participating private parks (many land near $25–35/night instead of $50–70), but availability is often capped to one or two nights per stay and blacked out in peak season — check each park's restrictions before counting on the discount.
  • A Thousand Trails zone membership carries an annual fee (commonly $500–700 for one zone) but drops each night inside the system to $0 beyond that fee — it only pencils out if you're realistically racking up 25+ nights a year at parks that participate.
  • Harvest Hosts memberships (roughly $100/year) get you free overnight parking at wineries, farms, and breweries with zero hookups — budget $20–40 per stop for the purchase that's the unwritten cost of admission, and don't expect water or sewer.
  • Boondocking on BLM or national forest land is genuinely free, but factor in the generator fuel, extra battery capacity, or solar setup you'll lean on without shore power, plus the dump station and water fill you'll pay for every 4–7 days.
  • State and national park campgrounds book out 6 months ahead for summer weekends — if your trip depends on cheaper public sites, reserve the moment the window opens or your budget quietly shifts toward pricier private parks with same-day availability.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do RV campgrounds cost per night?

Private RV parks and resorts with full hookups typically run $45–75 a night nationally, and can top $100 near national park entrances or on the coast in peak season. Public campgrounds — state parks, national forests, Corps of Engineers lakes — usually run $20–40 a night for a partial-hookup or no-hookup site. Free camping (BLM dispersed land, national forest dispersed sites, Harvest Hosts, some casino and big-box lots) costs $0 in fees but comes with no hookups.

Is a Thousand Trails or Passport America membership worth it?

It depends on your nights, not just the discount percentage. Passport America (roughly $44/year) cuts private-park rates about in half but is usually capped to a night or two per stay at each park, so it helps most if you're moving frequently between different private parks. Thousand Trails charges a much larger annual zone fee (often $500–700 for one zone) but drops most in-system nights to $0 — it tends to pay off only for people logging 25+ nights a year at participating parks, which fits full-timers and snowbirds more than a two-week vacation.

How do I budget for boondocking?

Boondocking removes the nightly camping fee, but not the cost — it shifts spending to generator fuel, extra battery or solar capacity, and the dump-and-fill stops you'll need every 4–7 days depending on tank size. Most boondocking RVers still budget one paid night (private park, $45–75) every few days specifically to dump tanks, fill fresh water, and run laundry.

What's the cost difference between state park and national park camping?

State park campgrounds usually run $20–35 a night and often include electric hookups. National park campgrounds tend to run $20–30 a night but rarely offer hookups at all, so you're relying on tank capacity or a generator during allowed hours. Both typically require booking through Recreation.gov or the state's own reservation system 3–6 months ahead for summer dates, and both usually cap consecutive-night stays around 14 days.

How accurate are the $55 and $30 default rates?

They're broad national averages, useful for a first-pass budget before you've booked anything. Real rates swing widely: a private park in a major metro area or near a national park gate can run $80–120, while a rural or off-season private park can be $35–45. Once you have a route, pull actual listed rates for 3–4 parks along it and adjust your private/public night split rather than trusting the defaults for a final number.
