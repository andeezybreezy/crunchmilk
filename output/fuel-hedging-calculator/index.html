Fuel Hedging Savings Calculator for Businesses

Calculate potential savings from fuel price hedging for your fleet or business

What This Fuel Hedging Calculator Actually Models

Fuel hedging means locking in a price today for fuel you'll buy later — through a futures contract, a forward purchase with a supplier, or a fuel surcharge formula — instead of paying whatever the spot market charges when the fuel is delivered. This calculator takes your monthly fuel use, your current spot price, the price a hedge would lock in, and a stress price you're worried about, and turns them into five numbers: total hedged cost, unhedged cost at the stress price, potential savings, the premium you pay to hedge, and the breakeven price where the hedge neither helps nor hurts. It's the arithmetic side of a hedging decision, not a substitute for the decision itself.

Why Diesel and Jet Fuel Volatility Hits Margins So Hard

For a trucking or delivery fleet, fuel commonly runs 20-25% of per-mile operating cost — larger than driver pay in some lanes and second only to labor overall. Airlines carry a similar exposure in jet fuel. Freight and passenger rates are usually set months in advance, often locked into contracts, so when spot diesel or jet fuel jumps 20-30% in a quarter, that cost increase has nowhere to go — it comes straight out of margin. Hedging exists to break that link: it swaps an unknown future fuel bill for a known one, at the cost of giving up any upside if prices fall instead. That's the whole tradeoff — protection against a spike, in exchange for possibly overpaying if the spike never comes.

A Worked Example: Hedging Half a Fleet's Volume

Take a fleet burning 50,000 gallons of diesel a month that hedges half its volume — 25,000 gallons — at a locked price of $3.80/gal, leaving the other 25,000 gallons exposed to spot. If spot then rises to $4.50/gal, the hedged gallons save $0.70 × 25,000 = $17,500 that month versus paying spot on the full volume. But if spot instead falls to $3.20/gal, that same hedge costs $0.60 × 25,000 = $15,000 in savings the fleet would have captured by staying unhedged. Neither outcome is a mistake — it's the nature of a hedge: it narrows the range of possible fuel bills, it doesn't guarantee the best one. Run your own volume, lock-in price, and a spike scenario you're actually worried about through the calculator above to see where your breakeven sits.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter your monthly fuel use in gallons: This is the volume the whole comparison scales from — use your fleet's actual average monthly diesel or jet fuel consumption, not a rounded guess.
  2. Enter your current spot price and the price a hedge would lock in: Current price is what you'd pay on the open market today; the hedge lock-in price is the fixed rate a broker, supplier, or surcharge formula would guarantee you for the contract period.
  3. Set a projected crisis price and hedge duration: The projected price is the spot spike you're stress-testing against — base it on a real scenario, like the highest price you saw in the last major runup. Duration is how many months the hedge covers.
  4. Click Calculate and read the five results: Check total hedged cost, unhedged cost at your stress price, potential savings, the hedge premium, and the breakeven price — then re-run with different hedge percentages or price scenarios before taking numbers to a broker or your CFO.

How It Works

This calculator runs the same three-line arithmetic a fuel broker or fleet controller uses to price a hedge: multiply gallons by price by duration, then compare the locked-in cost against what full market exposure would cost at a stress price you choose.

The basic rule:

  • Total hedged cost = monthly gallons × hedge price × months
  • Savings = unhedged cost - hedged cost
  • Premium = gallons × (hedge price - current price)

These numbers compare two cost scenarios — they are not a forecast of where diesel or jet fuel prices will actually go. Real hedge contracts also carry broker fees, margin requirements, and basis risk between the index price and what you pay at the pump. Talk to a commodities broker or your CFO/treasury advisor before signing anything.

Tips & Considerations

  • Set the projected crisis price to something you've actually seen, not a worst-case guess — pull the highest diesel or jet fuel price from the last 2-3 years so the comparison reflects real volatility, not fear.
  • Run the calculator at a few different hedge percentages of your total volume (25%, 50%, 100%) rather than assuming you should hedge everything — partial hedges cap worst-case cost while leaving room to benefit if prices drop.
  • Compare the hedge premium against the potential savings, not just the savings alone — a hedge that costs $0.10/gal to lock in versus a spike that would cost $1.70/gal is a very different trade than a hedge priced close to the stress scenario.
  • Remember the breakeven price is your hedge lock-in price itself — if you genuinely expect spot to stay below that level for the contract term, staying unhedged (or hedging a smaller share) may be the better call.
  • Treat this calculator as the arithmetic step, not the whole decision — bring your numbers to a commodities broker or your CFO/treasury advisor before entering an actual futures, forward, or surcharge contract, since real hedges carry fees, margin requirements, and basis risk this model doesn't include.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does fuel hedging actually mean for a fleet or logistics business?

It means locking in a price for fuel you haven't bought yet, instead of paying whatever the spot market charges on delivery day. You can lock in through diesel or jet fuel futures contracts, a forward purchase agreement with a supplier, or a fuel surcharge clause negotiated into customer contracts. Whichever route, you trade the chance of a lower future price for protection against a higher one — you're buying certainty, not a discount.

Can a fuel hedge actually lose a business money?

Yes, and that's the tradeoff this calculator is built to show. If you lock in at $3.90/gal and spot fuel later drops to $3.20/gal, you're still paying $3.90 — you gave up $0.70/gal in savings you would have gotten by staying exposed. Hedging doesn't guarantee the lowest price; it guarantees a known price. Whether that trade was worth it only shows up after the fact, once you know which way the market moved.

Who typically hedges fuel, and why?

Trucking and delivery fleets, airlines, marine shippers, and any business where fuel is a large, unpredictable share of operating cost. For a trucking company, diesel commonly runs 20-25% of per-mile operating cost — a spike that isn't hedged or surcharged can erase margin on contracts signed months earlier at a lower fuel assumption. Airlines hedge jet fuel for the same reason at much larger scale; Southwest's hedge book is the textbook case, worth billions during the 2008 oil spike and a drag on results when prices later fell.

What's the difference between hedging with futures and using a fuel surcharge?

A futures or forward contract locks a price with a financial counterparty or supplier — you owe that price regardless of what customers pay you. A fuel surcharge instead passes fuel cost swings through to customers via a formula tied to a published diesel index, so your exposure shifts to them (with a lag). Many fleets use both: a surcharge for pass-through protection on the revenue side, and a partial futures or forward hedge to smooth out the timing gap and the portion surcharges don't fully cover.

How much of our fuel volume should we hedge?

There's no universal answer — it's a risk tolerance and cash flow question for your CFO or treasury team, not a formula. Many fleets hedge 30-60% of expected volume rather than 100%, capping worst-case exposure while leaving room to benefit if prices fall. Run this calculator at a few different hedge percentages and hedge prices to see how the potential-savings and foregone-savings numbers move before deciding.

What is the breakeven fuel price telling me?

It's the spot price at which hedging comes out exactly even — which is your hedge lock-in price itself. If spot ends up above that price, the hedge saved you money; below it, staying unhedged would have been cheaper. The gap between your breakeven and where you actually think fuel is headed is the real bet you're making.

Does this calculator account for broker fees or margin requirements?

No — it isolates the fuel-price arithmetic (locked cost vs. spot-exposed cost) so you can see that tradeoff clearly. Real futures and forward contracts also involve transaction costs, potential margin calls if the market moves against an open position, and basis risk between the futures index and your local diesel price. Bring those specifics to a commodities broker before sizing an actual hedge.
