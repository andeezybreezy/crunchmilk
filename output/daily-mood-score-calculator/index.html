Daily Mood Tracker Score Calculator

Track and score your daily mood patterns

What the Daily Mood Tracker Score Calculator Does

Each day you rate five things you already notice about yourself — overall mood, anxiety, energy, sleep quality, and social connection — on a simple 1-10 scale, and the calculator turns those five numbers into one composite Wellness Score from 0-100. Anxiety is inverted in the formula (a rating of 3 counts as a positive contributor, not a negative one) so that lower anxiety pushes your score up rather than down, matching how the other four factors work. The result isn't a diagnosis; it's a single number you can log next to a date, the way you might log resting heart rate or hours slept, so a month of entries becomes a readable trend line instead of five separate columns of digits.

Why One Number Beats a Gut Feeling

Memory is a poor record-keeper for mood — ask most people how their week felt and they'll anchor on the worst or most recent day. A logged score doesn't have that bias. Say you record 6, 7, 5, 4, 6, 8, 7 across seven days: a simple average of about 6.1. On its own that's just a number, but plotted against sleep hours, workout days, or a menstrual cycle, patterns tend to surface fast — the 4 might land the morning after five hours of sleep, the 8 after a day with two social calls and a run. That's the real value of tracking: not the single-day score, but the correlations a week or a month of scores expose between mood and the things you can actually influence, like sleep timing, exercise, sunlight, and social contact.

How This Compares to Clinical Screening Tools

A daily 1-10 self-report isn't the same instrument as something like the WHO-5 Well-Being Index or PANAS (the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule), which use multiple standardized items and validated scoring to measure well-being or positive versus negative affect separately. This calculator is closer to a personal weather log: fast enough to fill in every day, specific enough to catch trends, but built for a different job than either of those instruments. Where a validated screener asks a fixed set of questions and scores them against published norms, this tool asks five questions you already understand intuitively and turns them into a trend you can watch over weeks and months.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Rate Your Overall Mood (1-10): Start with a gut-level number for how the day felt overall — 1 for a genuinely rough day, 10 for one of your best. Don't average it in your head; just answer for right now.
  2. Rate Anxiety, Energy, Sleep, and Social Connection: Fill in the remaining four 1-10 fields: how anxious you felt, your energy level, how well you slept the night before, and how connected you felt to other people today.
  3. Click Calculate: The calculator inverts your anxiety rating, adds all five numbers together, and scales the total to a 0-100 Wellness Score in your browser.
  4. Read the Score and Assessment, Then Log the Date: Note the score and short assessment alongside today's date somewhere you'll keep it — a notes app, spreadsheet, or journal — so tomorrow's entry has something to compare against.

How It Works

The Wellness Score folds five daily self-ratings into a single 0-100 number, weighting each factor equally and flipping the anxiety scale so that calm days count in the same direction as good sleep and high energy.

The basic rule:

  • Wellness Score — Score = (Mood + (11-Anxiety) + Energy + Sleep + Social) / 50 × 100 — Five-factor composite with anxiety inverted (lower is better).

Treat the score as a personal baseline, not a universal benchmark — a 62 might be a great day for one person and an off day for another. What matters most is how your own number moves week to week.

Tips & Considerations

  • Log at the same time each day — a 9pm entry and a 7am entry answer slightly different questions, and mixing them blurs the trend line.
  • Rate the day you just had, not the day you're hoping tomorrow will be — resist the urge to round a 5 up to a 7 out of optimism.
  • Don't overthink the number itself. The difference between a 6 and a 7 matters less than whether this week's scores are running lower than last month's.
  • Track for at least two to three weeks before drawing conclusions — a single bad day tells you almost nothing, but a sustained drop below your personal baseline does.
  • If anxiety and mood pull in opposite directions on the same day — say Anxiety 8 with Mood 6 — that's worth a closer look rather than letting the composite score smooth it over.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why invert the anxiety score in the formula?

Every other input in this calculator works the same direction — higher is better. Anxiety is naturally the opposite: a high anxiety rating is a bad sign, not a good one. Flipping it (11 minus your rating) before adding it in keeps all five numbers pointing the same way, so the composite score doesn't accidentally reward a stressful day.

Is a Wellness Score of 50 bad?

Not automatically. Fifty sits at the midpoint, and where your own scores tend to cluster matters more than any fixed cutoff. If your typical range is 45-60, a 50 is an ordinary day. If your usual range is 70-85, a run of scores near 50 is a meaningful drop worth paying attention to.

How is this different from apps that use PANAS or the WHO-5?

PANAS (the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule) and the WHO-5 Well-Being Index are validated multi-item questionnaires developed and tested for research and clinical screening, with published scoring thresholds. This calculator is a single quick composite built for personal day-to-day tracking — useful for spotting your own trends, but it hasn't gone through that validation process, and it isn't a screening instrument.

How many low-scoring days should worry me?

One or two low days is ordinary variance — poor sleep, a stressful meeting, or bad weather can all knock a few points off. A sustained drop below your personal baseline that lasts two weeks or more, especially alongside changes in sleep, appetite, or interest in things you usually enjoy, is a reasonable point to talk to a doctor or therapist rather than just keep tracking on your own.

Can I use this if I'm already in therapy or on medication?

Yes — plenty of people use a simple daily score alongside professional care specifically because it gives concrete numbers and dates to bring to a session, instead of trying to reconstruct how the last month felt purely from memory.

What if my mood and energy scores never seem to match?

That's common and informative rather than a problem — mood and energy are related but distinct. Low energy with a decent mood often points to a physical cause (sleep debt, illness, diet), while low mood with normal energy points more toward something situational or emotional. Tracking them as separate numbers, rather than blending them in your head, is exactly why the calculator asks for both.

Is this calculator a diagnostic or clinical tool?

No. It's a self-reflection and pattern-tracking tool, not a diagnostic instrument, and it won't tell you whether you have depression or an anxiety disorder. It's meant to complement, not replace, an assessment from a mental health professional — if low mood is persistent or is affecting your daily life, that's the right resource to reach out to.
