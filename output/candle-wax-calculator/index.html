Calculate exact wax, fragrance oil, and dye for your candles

Volume In, Weight Out: Why Wax Density Matters

Your jar is rated in fluid ounces or mL, but wax is bought, melted, and dosed with fragrance by weight — that mismatch is the whole reason a density conversion has to happen before you ever light a burner. Melted wax volume is fairly consistent within a type, but density swings across types because the raw material is different: soy wax runs about 0.86 oz per fl oz of melted volume (≈ 0.90 g/mL), paraffin sits closer to 0.90 oz per fl oz (≈ 0.93 g/mL) though additive-heavy container blends with vybar or stearic acid run a touch denser, coconut wax blends are the lightest common option at roughly 0.82 oz per fl oz (≈ 0.85 g/mL), and beeswax is the heaviest of the four at about 0.96 oz per fl oz (≈ 0.96 g/mL). Swap wax types in the same jar and the fill line on the glass does not move, but the number on your scale does — sometimes by more than 10%.

Worked Example: One 8 fl oz Jar, Four Waxes

Take a standard 8 fl oz tumbler filled to 85%, which is the usual headspace for a wick and a clean pour: 8 × 0.85 = 6.8 fl oz of wax actually goes in. In soy that's 6.8 × 0.86 ≈ 5.85 oz of wax per candle. Pour the identical jar in paraffin and it's 6.8 × 0.90 ≈ 6.12 oz. Switch to beeswax and it climbs to 6.8 × 0.96 ≈ 6.53 oz — nearly 0.7 oz heavier than the soy pour for the exact same glass. Coconut wax comes in lightest at 6.8 × 0.82 ≈ 5.58 oz. Run twelve of these jars and that soy-to-beeswax gap turns into over 8 oz of wax, which matters when you're pricing a batch or estimating whether a 5 lb bag will clear an order. A recipe card written for soy will not hand you the right number for beeswax — you have to reconvert through density, not just swap the wax name.

Fill Percentage, Shrinkage, and the Second Pour

Fill percentage is not a safety margin you can round away — 85–90% leaves headspace for the wick tab and stops hot wax from creeping over the rim during the melt pool. But even a correctly filled jar rarely finishes flush. Soy wax in particular contracts as it crystallizes, and a single pour often leaves a shallow crater or pull-away ring around the wick as it cools. The usual fix is a second, thinner top-off pour: reserve roughly 10% of the batch, reheat it to a thin, pourable consistency once the first pour has mostly set, and skim it over the surface. Paraffin behaves differently in glass — it's prone to wet spots, patchy areas where the wax pulls away from the container wall as it shrinks, which is a cosmetic issue rather than a performance one. Soy is more prone to frosting, the white crystalline bloom that shows up on the surface or sides as the wax re-crystallizes; it's cosmetic too, and slower, more even cooling reduces it but rarely eliminates it entirely.

How It Works

Wax quantity comes down to one conversion: turn the fluid volume your container actually holds into a weight, using the density of whichever wax you're pouring. Fragrance oil and dye then ride along as percentages of that wax weight, not the container volume — so the density step has to happen first or every number after it is off.

The basic rule:

  • Wax weight (oz) = container volume (fl oz) × fill % ÷ 100 × wax density (oz per fl oz)
  • Soy wax density ≈ 0.86 oz per fl oz of melted volume (≈ 0.90 g/mL)
  • Paraffin wax density ≈ 0.90 oz per fl oz (≈ 0.93 g/mL) — additive-heavy container blends run slightly denser
  • Coconut wax blends ≈ 0.82 oz per fl oz (≈ 0.85 g/mL), the lightest of the common blends
  • Beeswax ≈ 0.96 oz per fl oz (≈ 0.96 g/mL), the densest of the four
  • Fragrance oil (oz) = total wax weight × fragrance load % ÷ 100
  • Dye (approximate) ≈ 0.1 oz of dye block per pound of wax for a medium shade

The weight this calculator hands you is the raw wax to melt, not what ends up in the finished candle — shrinkage during cooling and any wax left behind on your pouring pitcher or thermometer both eat into that number slightly. Melt a little more than the total, expect to reserve part of it for a top-off pour once soy or a soy blend pulls away from the wick, and keep the extra on hand rather than melting your batch down to the last gram. Weigh wax on a scale rather than trusting the container's marketed ounce rating, too — jar manufacturers round, and a "9 oz tin" is a labeling convention, not a measurement you can calculate against.

Tips & Considerations

  • Weigh your empty container and lid before you start, then weigh the finished candle — the difference tells you your real fill weight and lets you sanity-check the calculator against your own scale.
  • Buy roughly 10% more wax than the calculator's total — shrinkage, a top-off pour, and wax left on stir sticks and pitchers all eat into your usable amount.
  • If your soy candles keep frosting, it's the wax re-crystallizing, not a mistake — cooling the poured candles more slowly and evenly (skip the fridge, avoid drafts) reduces it, but a soy blend with stearic acid resists it better if it bothers you.
  • Wet spots on paraffin in glass are cosmetic, not a burn hazard — pouring a few degrees hotter or preheating the jar can reduce them, but they're a known trait of the wax, not a sign you did something wrong.
  • When switching wax type mid-project, don't just swap the label — beeswax runs about 12% heavier by weight than soy in the same container, so re-run the numbers before you order stock.
  • Melt point isn't the same as pour temperature — check your specific wax's data sheet, since coconut blends can be workable well below 100°F while beeswax often needs the low 180s°F just to fully liquefy.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much wax do I actually need for one candle?

Multiply your container's usable volume (fill percentage applied) by the density of your wax type. An 8 fl oz jar at 85% fill in soy works out to 8 × 0.85 × 0.86 ≈ 5.85 oz. The same jar in beeswax needs about 6.53 oz because beeswax is denser — same glass, different weight on the scale.

Why does the same jar need different amounts of wax for different wax types?

Because weight and volume aren't interchangeable once density changes. Soy wax measures about 0.86 oz per fl oz of melted volume, paraffin about 0.90, coconut blends about 0.82, and beeswax about 0.96. A jar's fluid-ounce capacity is fixed, but the weight needed to fill it moves with whichever wax you pick — that's the whole reason this calculator asks for wax type rather than assuming one.

Should I fill my container all the way to the rim?

No — leave headspace, typically by filling to 85–90% of the container's rated volume. That gap accommodates the wick tab, keeps hot wax from creeping over the edge as the melt pool forms, and gives you room for a clean top-off pour if the first pour leaves a sink hole.

Why does my candle look sunk in or cratered near the wick after it cools?

That's shrinkage, most common with soy wax as it crystallizes and contracts while cooling. It's not a failed batch — reserve about 10% of your total wax, reheat it to a thin, pourable state once the first pour has mostly set, and pour a thin top-off layer over the surface to level it out.

How much dye do I need per pound of wax?

For dye blocks, start around 0.1 oz per pound of wax for a medium shade and adjust from there — soy tends to hold color lighter than paraffin at the same dose because it's more opaque. For liquid dye, start with 3–10 drops per pound. Add gradually; it's much easier to deepen a color than to lighten one after the fact.

What's actually different between soy, paraffin, coconut, and beeswax beyond density?

Soy is plant-based, burns with less soot, and melts lowest of the four (roughly 113–127°F), which makes it forgiving for container work but prone to frosting. Paraffin is petroleum-based, holds fragrance and color well, and melts hotter (130–150°F), but is the one most prone to wet spots in glass. Coconut wax blends are the lightest and lowest-melting (around 100–107°F), popular for strong scent throw but usually pricier. Beeswax is the densest and highest-melting of the four (144–149°F), burns slowly with a natural honey scent, but holds the least added fragrance of the group.
