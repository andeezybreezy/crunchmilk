Sea Level Rise Calculator

Estimate flood risk from projected sea level rise

What This Sea Level Rise Calculator Measures

This calculator answers a narrow, specific question: given a property's elevation above current mean sea level, how much of that elevation buffer survives a chosen emissions scenario by a chosen target year between 2030 and 2100? Enter elevation in feet, pick one of four pathways — Low (about 1 foot of global rise by 2100), Moderate (2 feet), High (4 feet), or Extreme (6.5+ feet) — and a target year, and the tool interpolates linearly from a 2020 baseline to estimate the projected rise, the resulting safety margin, and a qualitative risk level. It's a screening tool for thinking through relative exposure across scenarios, not a substitute for a FEMA flood elevation certificate, a NOAA Sea Level Rise Viewer report, or a licensed coastal engineer's site assessment.

Global Average vs. What Happens at Your Coastline

Global mean sea level, tracked by satellite altimetry since 1993, is currently rising at roughly 3.3-4mm per year — up from about 1.4mm/year across most of the 20th century, and the rate keeps climbing as ice-sheet loss accelerates. Two physical processes drive essentially all of that rise: thermal expansion, where ocean water occupies more volume as it absorbs heat (the ocean has taken up more than 90% of the extra energy trapped by greenhouse gases), and the melting of land ice — mountain glaciers, the Greenland ice sheet, and the Antarctic ice sheet — which adds new water mass to the ocean. But the figure that matters for any one coastline is local relative sea level, not the global average, and the two can differ by a factor of two or more. Vertical land motion is the biggest wedge: the Gulf Coast around New Orleans is subsiding several millimeters to over a centimeter per year from groundwater withdrawal, oil and gas extraction, and sediment compaction, roughly doubling or tripling the effective local rise on top of the global signal, while parts of Alaska and Scandinavia are still rebounding from the weight of ice-age glaciers and see local sea level rise more slowly than the global average — in a few spots it's actually falling relative to land. Ocean currents matter too: a stretch of the US East Coast from Cape Hatteras to Boston has become a documented 'hotspot' where relative sea level has risen faster than the global mean, linked in part to a weakening Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation. This calculator uses global scenario figures as its baseline; if you're in a subsidence zone like coastal Louisiana or Houston, treat the output as a floor, not a ceiling.

Reading Your Safety Margin — A Worked Example

Take a property sitting at 6 feet of elevation above current mean sea level, the Moderate Emissions scenario selected (2 feet of global rise by 2100, broadly consistent with IPCC AR6's SSP2-4.5 middle-of-the-road pathway), and a target year of 2060. Because the calculator interpolates linearly from a 2020 baseline, the 40-year mark works out to roughly 1.0 foot (≈0.3m) of projected rise — leaving about 5.0 feet of safety margin, which reads as low risk. Push the same scenario out to 2100 and the full 2 feet of rise cuts that margin to 4 feet; switch to High Emissions (4 feet by 2100) at the same target year and the margin shrinks to just 2 feet. That gradual erosion of margin over decades is the story this tool tells — but it's only half the picture. A single storm can add 3 to 15+ feet of surge on top of whatever the baseline sea level happens to be that day, so a property with a comfortable 4- or 5-foot margin against the slow trend can still flood in a single hurricane, king tide, or nor'easter, decades before gradual sea level rise alone would ever close that gap. Read this calculator's risk level as a baseline-inundation signal, and treat storm surge as the separate, faster-moving hazard it actually is.

  2. Select a Climate Scenario: Choose Low, Moderate, High, or Extreme emissions, corresponding to roughly 1, 2, 4, and 6.5+ feet of global sea level rise by 2100. These track NOAA's and IPCC AR6's published scenario framework — Low and Moderate align with stronger near-term mitigation, High and Extreme with continued high emissions and faster-than-expected ice-sheet loss.
  3. Set Your Target Year: Pick a year between 2030 and 2100. Near-term years show scenarios converging on a similar figure, since much of that rise is already locked in; later years show the scenarios pulling apart as cumulative emissions diverge.

How It Works

This calculator converts a chosen emissions scenario and target year into an estimated elevation buffer for a specific property, using the same reference framework coastal planners use to compare scenarios side by side.

The basic rule:

  • Projected rise is interpolated linearly between a 2020 baseline (0 additional feet) and each scenario's 2100 endpoint — a simplification of a curve that in reality accelerates rather than climbs in a straight line, especially under higher-emissions pathways.
  • The four scenario options roughly track NOAA's Interagency Sea Level Rise Technical Report and IPCC AR6: Low (≈1 ft / ≈0.3m by 2100, near SSP1-2.6), Moderate (≈2 ft / ≈0.6m, near SSP2-4.5), High (≈4 ft / ≈1.2m, near SSP3-7.0 with elevated ice-sheet loss), and Extreme (6.5+ ft / ≈2m, the low-probability, high-impact ice-cliff-instability storyline).
  • Safety margin is simply elevation minus projected rise at the target year; risk level buckets that margin into qualitative tiers, but it does not include storm surge, king tides, wave run-up, or local drainage capacity.
  • Storm surge is a separate hazard from baseline sea level rise and can temporarily add 3 to 15+ feet during a hurricane or major coastal storm — short-term flood risk at most properties is usually dominated by storm events, not by the gradual creep this calculator estimates.

These are scenario projections built on published climate science, not a prediction for any specific address. Actual local sea level rise depends on land subsidence or uplift, regional ocean currents, and which global emissions pathway the world actually follows — for a property-specific flood determination or an insurance or zoning decision, consult NOAA's Sea Level Rise Viewer, your local floodplain management office, or a licensed coastal engineer.

Tips & Considerations

  • Use your most precise elevation figure — NAVD88 orthometric height from a survey or FEMA elevation certificate — rather than a rough map estimate; a one- or two-foot error in elevation can flip a result from low to moderate risk.
  • Run the same elevation against all four emissions scenarios before settling on one number — the spread between Low and Extreme by 2100 is several feet, and that range is itself useful information for long-horizon decisions.
  • Treat the safety margin as a baseline-inundation buffer only — for any coastal property, check storm surge exposure separately, since a single storm event can temporarily exceed the gradual sea level rise this tool projects all the way out to 2100.
  • If you're in a region with known land subsidence — the Gulf Coast, parts of the Chesapeake Bay, the San Joaquin Delta — expect your true local rise to run faster than the global scenario figures this calculator uses as its baseline.
  • For anything beyond casual scenario comparison — a purchase decision, an insurance question, a permitting or zoning matter — cross-check results against NOAA's Sea Level Rise Viewer and your local floodplain management office, which use higher-resolution, locally calibrated data.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much will sea levels actually rise?

Global mean sea level is currently rising about 3.3-4mm per year based on satellite altimetry, and IPCC AR6 projects roughly 0.3-0.6m (about 1-2 feet) of additional global rise by 2100 under low-to-moderate emissions scenarios, and 0.6-1.0m or more (roughly 2-3.5+ feet) under high-emissions pathways — with a low-probability tail risk near 2m if ice sheet loss accelerates faster than the central estimate. At least a foot of rise is considered effectively locked in from ocean heat already absorbed and ice already committed to melting, even under aggressive mitigation.

Why does my local sea level rise differently from the global average?

Because the global figure is an average across an ocean that isn't rising uniformly. Land itself moves — subsiding several millimeters to over a centimeter per year in places like coastal Louisiana from groundwater extraction and sediment compaction, or rebounding upward in parts of Alaska and Scandinavia still recovering from ice-age glacial unloading. Ocean currents and even the gravitational pull of large ice sheets shift water distribution regionally, which is part of why a stretch of the US East Coast has seen faster-than-average relative sea level rise in recent decades.

Does this calculator account for storm surge or king tides?

No — the projected rise and safety margin reflect gradual, long-term baseline sea level only. Storm surge from hurricanes or major coastal storms can temporarily add 3 to 15+ feet on top of that baseline, and king tides can add another 1-2 feet during especially high astronomical tides. These short-term events are usually a bigger and more immediate flood risk than the gradual trend this tool projects, so treat them as a separate hazard layer on top of the baseline result.

Which emissions scenario should I use?

There's no single correct choice — comparing scenarios is the point. Moderate (SSP2-4.5-like, roughly 2 feet by 2100) is often used as a reasonable central planning case; many coastal engineering guidelines now recommend also checking the High scenario for critical infrastructure or long-lived assets, since underestimating rise is usually far costlier than over-preparing for it.

How reliable is a straight-line interpolation between 2020 and 2100?

It's a simplification. Real sea level rise curves accelerate rather than climb in a straight line, particularly under higher-emissions scenarios where ice-sheet contributions grow nonlinearly over the century — so a linear model tends to slightly overstate near-term rise and understate rise in the final decades relative to detailed process-based models. It stays useful for quick scenario comparison, but a precise mid-century estimate should reference NOAA's or IPCC's full projection curves rather than a straight line.

Is this a substitute for an official flood zone determination or elevation certificate?

No. This tool estimates relative exposure across climate scenarios for planning and comparison purposes. It does not replace a FEMA Flood Insurance Rate Map determination, a licensed surveyor's elevation certificate, or a coastal engineer's site-specific hazard assessment — all of which draw on local data this calculator does not have access to.
