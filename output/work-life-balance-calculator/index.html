Work-Life Balance Calculator

Score your work-life balance across key areas

What the Five Numbers Actually Represent

Work-life balance research doesn't hinge on a single hours-worked figure — it hinges on the ratio between demand and recovery. This calculator asks for five inputs — weekly work hours, exercise days, social and hobby hours, nightly sleep, and vacation days used — because each one stands in for a distinct dimension that occupational-health literature ties to burnout risk: workload relative to recovery capacity, physical resilience, relational investment, nightly restoration, and your demonstrated ability to disengage from work. None of these numbers means much alone. Someone working 48 hours a week who sleeps 8 hours, exercises four times, and actually takes vacation is in a very different position than someone working those same 48 hours while sleeping 5 hours and never taking a day off. The composite score exists specifically to catch that difference — a weighted sum across five dimensions, not a single-axis hours tally, because a single-axis tally misses the cases where people work reasonable hours but still burn out from poor recovery, or work long hours but protect themselves well enough to stay functional.

Why Composite Scoring Beats a Single Hours-Worked Number

The job demands-control model and the effort-recovery model — two of the better-supported frameworks in occupational psychology — point to the same conclusion: burnout isn't produced by hours alone, it's produced by the combination of high demand, low control over how and when work happens, and insufficient recovery between exposures. A person working 55 hours a week who has real autonomy over their schedule, protects evenings for family, and takes every vacation day they're owed often reports better well-being than someone working 42 hours under a manager who texts at 9pm and treats unused vacation as a badge of honor. That's why this tool weights several inputs instead of grading you primarily on hours. It's a rough proxy, not a clinical instrument, but it's built to approximate the same multi-factor logic researchers use rather than reducing an entire week to one number.

Reading Your Score — A Worked Example

Take a hypothetical case: a 36-year-old operations manager working 55 hours a week, exercising once, spending about 4 hours on hobbies and friends, sleeping 6 hours a night, and having used only 4 of her vacation days this year. Run those through the five brackets and you get roughly 6 + 8 + 6 + 8 + 1 = 29 out of 100. No single number in that list looks catastrophic on its own — 55 hours is demanding but not extreme, 6 hours of sleep is short but not rare, 4 vacation days feels low but plenty of people land there. The composite score is what reveals the real picture: every dimension is simultaneously under-resourced, which is a materially different situation than scoring 29 because one dimension collapsed while the others stayed strong. That pattern — several moderate deficits stacking rather than one severe deficit — is exactly the profile occupational-health researchers associate with gradual, easy-to-miss burnout, because no single red flag ever gets loud enough on its own to prompt a change.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter Your Actual Weekly Hours: Start with the hours you actually worked last week, not your contracted or ideal number — include time spent on messages or calls outside your normal schedule, since that's part of what this score is trying to capture.
  2. Add Your Recovery and Boundary Inputs: Fill in exercise days, social/hobby hours, nightly sleep, and vacation days used over the past year. These four fields are what let the score distinguish 'busy but recovering well' from 'busy and running on empty.'
  3. Calculate Your Composite Score: Click Calculate to score each of the five dimensions and combine them into a single result out of 100, along with the one dimension pulling your score down the most.
  4. Identify and Act on Your Weakest Dimension: Don't just note the overall score — look at which single dimension is lowest, since that's usually the most specific and most fixable lever, whether it's a boundary to set, a sleep habit to adjust, or vacation days to actually book.

How It Works

This calculator doesn't run on one formula — it scores five separate dimensions tied to recovery and burnout research, then adds them into a single composite out of 100. Each dimension is scored on its own below, so you can see exactly which part of your week is dragging the total down instead of getting one undifferentiated number.

The basic rule:

  • Weekly work hours (recovery-capacity proxy): 35–40 hrs ≈ 25 pts, 45 hrs ≈ 18 pts, 50 hrs ≈ 12 pts, 55 hrs ≈ 6 pts, 60+ hrs ≈ 2 pts — the score drops fastest right around the 50-hour mark, close to where several studies find output-per-hour starts falling and recovery windows start shrinking.
  • Exercise days per week (physical-resilience proxy): 4+ days ≈ 25 pts, 2–3 days ≈ 15 pts, 1 day ≈ 8 pts, 0 days ≈ 3 pts — regular movement is one of the more consistent physiological buffers against sustained stress load.
  • Social and hobby hours per week (relational-investment proxy): 10+ hrs ≈ 20 pts, 5–9 hrs ≈ 13 pts, 1–4 hrs ≈ 6 pts, 0 hrs ≈ 0 pts — time spent on relationships and non-work identity protects against work becoming the only thing left in your week.
  • Sleep hours per night (nightly-recovery proxy): 7.5+ hrs ≈ 20 pts, 7 hrs ≈ 15 pts, 6 hrs ≈ 8 pts, under 6 hrs ≈ 2 pts — sleep debt accumulates across a work week in a way that a single rough night doesn't.
  • Vacation days used per year (disengagement-capacity proxy): 15+ days ≈ 10 pts, 10–14 days ≈ 7 pts, 5–9 days ≈ 4 pts, under 5 days ≈ 1 pt — days accrued but never taken function less like a benefit and more like an unpaid obligation sitting on the calendar.

These brackets are approximations drawn from patterns in occupational-health and sleep research, not a diagnostic formula calibrated to your job, industry, or personal history. Two people can land on the identical total and still be in very different situations — someone coming out of a short, acute project crunch looks different on paper than someone in a role that's been unsustainable for two years, even if today's five numbers match exactly. Treat the score as a prompt to look closer at whichever dimension came in lowest, not as a verdict on your week.

Tips & Considerations

  • If your work-hours score is the lowest of the five, the fix usually isn't 'work less' in the abstract — it's naming one specific boundary, like a fixed stop time three days a week or routing after-hours messages to voicemail, and testing whether it holds for two weeks before renegotiating further.
  • A low vacation-days score paired with a decent hours score often means the job is fine on paper but your ability to actually disengage isn't — check whether you're skipping vacation because of workload, guilt, or a culture where no one takes their days, since each has a different fix.
  • Watch for the pattern where sleep and social/hobby hours drop together — that combination, more than hours worked alone, is one of the more reliable early signals of burnout building before people consciously notice it.
  • If autonomy over your schedule feels low — meetings booked over your calendar without asking, no say in when deep work happens — that's a control-over-demands problem the hours number won't capture; naming it specifically to a manager tends to land better than describing it as general overwhelm.
  • Re-run the calculator after any specific change you make — a new boundary, a declined project, a schedule shift — rather than relying on memory of how things felt. A repeat score makes it much easier to tell whether the change actually worked.

Frequently Asked Questions

What actually predicts burnout better than hours worked alone?

The combination of high demands and low control over your schedule tends to predict burnout better than hours in isolation. Someone working long hours with real autonomy over when and how they work, plus solid recovery time, often reports better well-being than someone working moderate hours under constant after-hours contact with no say over their own calendar. That's why this calculator scores five dimensions together rather than grading you mainly on hours worked.

What's a reasonable range for each of the five inputs?

Roughly: 35–45 weekly work hours, 3 or more exercise days, 8+ social/hobby hours, 7.5+ hours of sleep, and using most or all of your vacation days in a year. These are population-level patterns, not fixed rules — someone in a physically demanding job may need less structured exercise, and some people genuinely function on a bit less sleep. Use the brackets as a reference point rather than a number to hit exactly.

How do I actually fix a low score without quitting my job?

Target whichever single dimension scored lowest instead of trying to overhaul everything at once. A boundary problem — constant after-hours messages — needs a stated boundary, like a fixed stop time three days a week or routing work messages away from your personal phone after a set hour. A recovery problem — low sleep, low exercise, unused vacation — usually needs a scheduling change rather than willpower: block that time the same way you'd block a meeting, and protect it as firmly.

Is this the same as a clinical burnout assessment?

No. This is a self-assessment built from general research patterns across five dimensions, not a clinical burnout or mental health diagnosis, and it hasn't been validated the way a clinical screening tool would be. If you're dealing with persistent exhaustion, a loss of interest in things you used to enjoy, or distress that isn't easing up, it's worth talking to a healthcare professional, a therapist, or your employer's Employee Assistance Program (EAP) if one is available. A low score here is a reason to look closer, not a diagnosis.

How often should I recalculate?

Recalculate after any deliberate change — a new boundary, a declined project, a role or schedule shift — and otherwise every few months as a general check-in. Work-life balance tends to drift gradually, often unnoticed until one dimension, like sleep or vacation use, has been low for months on end. Checking periodically catches that drift earlier than waiting for something to feel obviously wrong.
