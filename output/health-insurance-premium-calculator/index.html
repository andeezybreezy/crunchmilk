Health Insurance Premium Calculator

Estimate your ACA marketplace health insurance premium

What This Calculator Actually Estimates

This tool approximates what an ACA marketplace plan might cost before you ever open a real quote. Marketplace premiums are priced around four metal tiers — Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Platinum — which describe actuarial value, the share of an average enrollee's medical costs the plan covers, not the quality of the doctors in its network. Bronze plans target roughly 60% actuarial value and carry the lowest premium alongside the highest deductible; Silver sits near 70%; Gold around 80%; Platinum near 90%, each step trading a higher monthly bill for lower costs when you actually use care. The calculator starts from a baseline rate for your chosen tier, applies ACA age-rating — insurers can charge a 64-year-old up to 3× what a 21-year-old pays for the identical plan in the identical area — and then subtracts an estimated premium tax credit based on your household income, landing on a net premium that approximates what would show up on your bill.

How the Premium Tax Credit Changes the Number

The subsidy math runs off your income expressed as a percentage of the federal poverty level (FPL) for your household size, compared against a sliding applicable-percentage scale — lower income relative to FPL means a smaller expected contribution and a bigger credit. Take a single 40-year-old earning $43,000, about 275% of the FPL used for 2026 coverage. If the benchmark (second-lowest-cost) Silver plan in their county runs $560/month and their applicable percentage at that income band works out to roughly 6%, their expected contribution caps at $43,000 × 6% ÷ 12 ≈ $215/month, making the subsidy about $560 − $215 = $345/month. Enrolling in an actual Silver plan priced at $530/month brings the net premium to $530 − $345 = $185/month, or roughly $2,220 for the year. That same $345 subsidy applies just as well to a cheaper Bronze plan or a pricier Gold plan — only the gap it needs to close changes.

Why Your Real Marketplace Quote Will Differ

Every number above is a planning estimate, not an actual insurance quote. Premiums are filed separately by each insurer, for each plan, within county-level rating areas, so two people with the same age and income in neighboring counties can see meaningfully different benchmark prices and subsidy amounts — and some rural counties have only one or two insurers filing plans at all. States that allow it can also add a tobacco-use surcharge of up to 50%, and whether your state expanded Medicaid changes who's even subsidy-eligible at the low end of the income scale. Use this calculator to get oriented on the mechanics — age rating, actuarial value, and the income-based credit — then confirm exact figures against the actual plans listed for your ZIP code at healthcare.gov or your state's exchange before you enroll or set a budget.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter your projected annual household income: Use your expected Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) for the coverage year, not a past pay stub — this figure, converted into a percentage of the federal poverty level for your household size, is what the premium tax credit formula runs on.
  2. Enter your age: ACA rules let insurers rate adults on a 3:1 curve, meaning a plan can cost up to three times as much at 64 as it does at 21 for identical coverage. This input drives that age-rating multiplier on the gross premium.
  3. Enter family size and choose a metal tier: Family size shifts the federal poverty level threshold used for the subsidy calculation and adds each additional adult's own age-rated premium. Picking Bronze, Silver, Gold, or Platinum sets the baseline rate tied to that tier's actuarial value — about 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90% respectively.
  4. Review the gross premium, subsidy, and net premium: The gross premium is the sticker price before any credit; the subsidy is the estimated premium tax credit; the net premium is roughly what you'd actually owe monthly. Treat the net figure as a starting point, then verify it against real plans for your county at healthcare.gov.

How It Works

The calculator works backward from the two levers that actually move an ACA marketplace bill: age and income. It starts with a baseline sticker premium for the metal tier you select, then applies an age-rating multiplier — insurers are permitted to charge their oldest enrollees (64) up to 3× what they charge their youngest adult enrollees (21) for the identical plan in the identical rating area, scaled smoothly by age in between. Family size adds each additional enrollee's own age-rated cost rather than a flat multiplier, which is how real marketplace billing works for adults (children are rated differently and the ACA caps how much a plan can count toward the total for the third and later child). Once the gross (sticker) premium is set, the tool estimates a premium tax credit by comparing your household income, expressed as a percentage of the federal poverty level (FPL), against a sliding applicable-percentage scale — the lower your income sits relative to FPL, the smaller the share of it you're expected to put toward a benchmark Silver plan, and the larger the subsidy that covers the rest.

The basic rule:

  • Gross premium — Gross premium = Tier base rate × Age-rating factor (up to 3× from age 21 to 64) + additional family members' own age-rated premiums — Each metal tier has its own base rate because it targets a different actuarial value — the share of an average population's medical costs the plan picks up: about 60% for Bronze, 70% for Silver, 80% for Gold, and 90% for Platinum. Higher actuarial value means a higher gross premium and lower cost-sharing (deductibles, copays) when you use care.
  • Premium tax credit (subsidy) — Subsidy ≈ Benchmark Silver premium − (Household income × Applicable % ÷ 12) — The applicable percentage rises as your income climbs further above 100% of the federal poverty level — it is not a flat rate. The resulting dollar subsidy is fixed for the year and can be applied to any metal tier, not just Silver, which is why picking a cheaper Bronze plan with the same subsidy can sometimes bring the net premium close to $0, while applying it to a pricier Gold plan narrows but doesn't erase the gap.
  • Net premium — Net premium = Gross premium − Subsidy (floored at $0) — This is the number that would actually appear on a monthly invoice if the underlying inputs matched a real plan exactly — which, given regional pricing differences, they usually won't.

Everything above is a planning estimate, not an actual quote. Real ACA marketplace premiums are set at the county and insurer level — two people with identical age and income living a few counties apart can see meaningfully different benchmark premiums and subsidy amounts. Before budgeting around a number, confirm it against the actual plans listed for your ZIP code at healthcare.gov or your state's own exchange.

Tips & Considerations

  • Base your income entry on this year's projected MAGI, not last year's return — marketplace subsidies reconcile against actual income at tax time, and a large gap between your estimate and reality can mean repaying part of the credit or being owed more.
  • If you're subsidy-eligible, price out a Silver plan even if you'd normally lean Bronze: only Silver plans unlock additional cost-sharing reductions on deductibles and copays for incomes roughly under 200–250% of the federal poverty level.
  • Because the subsidy is a fixed dollar amount tied to the benchmark Silver plan, applying it to a cheaper Bronze plan can sometimes push the net premium close to $0 — worth checking before assuming Silver is always the better deal.
  • If you're under 30 and generally healthy, compare a catastrophic plan against Bronze — catastrophic plans skip most subsidies but carry very low premiums and may suit someone mainly protecting against a worst-case medical bill.
  • A change in hours, a new job, a divorce, or a new dependent all count as qualifying life events — update your marketplace application promptly rather than waiting for the next open enrollment, since both your subsidy and your plan options can shift.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the subsidy depend on my income as a percent of the federal poverty level, not just my raw income?

Because the ACA's sliding-scale formula is built around the federal poverty level (FPL) for your household size, not a dollar income bracket. For a single adult, 100% of FPL is roughly $15,000 to $15,700 depending on the year (add roughly $5,000 to $5,500 per additional household member). Your subsidy eligibility and the applicable percentage you're expected to contribute are both set by where your income falls on that FPL scale — so a $40,000 income means something very different for a household of one versus a household of four.

Which plan tier should I pick — Bronze, Silver, Gold, or Platinum?

It depends on how much care you expect to use and whether you qualify for cost-sharing reductions. Bronze (about 60% actuarial value) has the lowest premium and the highest deductible, which can suit someone healthy who mainly wants protection from a worst-case bill. Silver (about 70%) is the tier that matters most if you're subsidy-eligible, because premium tax credits are calculated off the benchmark Silver plan, and only Silver plans unlock additional cost-sharing reductions for incomes under roughly 200–250% of FPL. Gold (about 80%) and Platinum (about 90%) trade a higher monthly premium for lower costs when you actually see a doctor — worth it if you have ongoing prescriptions, a chronic condition, or expect surgery.

Can you walk through an actual numbers example?

Take a single 40-year-old earning $43,000 a year, which is about 275% of the 2025 FPL used for 2026 coverage (roughly $15,650 for one person). Suppose the second-lowest-cost Silver plan (the benchmark) in their county runs $560/month, and at 275% of FPL their applicable percentage works out to about 6% of income. That caps their expected contribution at $43,000 × 6% ÷ 12 ≈ $215/month, so the monthly subsidy is about $560 − $215 = $345. If they actually enroll in a Silver plan priced at $530/month, their net premium is $530 − $345 = $185/month, or about $2,220 for the year — even though the plan's full sticker price was nearly three times that after subsidy.

Why would my real quote differ from this estimate?

This tool uses a simplified, single baseline rate per tier and a generic applicable-percentage curve. Actual marketplace premiums are filed separately by every insurer, for every plan, in every county-level rating area, so two neighboring counties can show different benchmark premiums, different insurer line-ups, or different subsidy amounts. Tobacco use can also add a surcharge of up to 50% in states that allow it, and your state may run cost-sharing reduction or subsidy rules on top of the federal baseline. Treat this as a starting point for budgeting, then confirm exact numbers against plans available for your ZIP code at healthcare.gov or your state exchange.

What income figure should I enter?

Use your projected Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) for the coverage year, not last year's W-2 — marketplace subsidies are based on an estimate of this year's income, reconciled against your actual MAGI when you file taxes. If you underestimate and your income comes in higher than projected, you may have to repay part of the subsidy at tax time; if you overestimate, you may be owed additional credit. Updating your income estimate on the marketplace promptly after a raise, job change, or new contract work helps avoid a surprise reconciliation bill.
