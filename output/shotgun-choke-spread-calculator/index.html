Shotgun Choke Pattern Calculator

Estimate shot pattern spread by choke and distance

What This Calculator Estimates

Every shotshell leaves the muzzle as a single wad of shot, and choke constriction is what governs how quickly that column opens into a spreading cone downrange. Enter the choke you're shooting — Cylinder, Improved Cylinder, Modified, Improved Modified, or Full — together with your distance in yards, and this tool returns the two numbers every wingshooter and turkey hunter cares about: pattern spread (the diameter of the shot column at that range) and pattern density (roughly what share of your pellets land inside the standard 30" circle used to score patterns on a board). Cylinder bore, with essentially no constriction, throws the widest, thinnest pattern — useful over a flushing dog at 15-20 yards but hopeless at 45. Full choke squeezes the column down to roughly two-thirds its unconstricted diameter, holding pellets together far enough downrange to kill a turkey at 40 yards or break a true pair on report.

Why Spread and Density Interact

Take a Full choke at 40 yards, the classic pattern-board reference distance: using a spread factor of about 0.5 inches per yard, the shot column runs roughly 20" across — tight enough that a turkey's head and neck rarely escape it — with density around 70% of the pellets landing inside the 30" scoring circle. Swap to a Cylinder bore at the same 40 yards and the math flips: spread widens to roughly 40", while density drops to around 40%, meaning well over half the charge scatters outside the circle entirely. Run a Modified choke at 40 yards and you land in between, at roughly 28" of spread with density in the 55-60% range — the reason Modified is the default general-purpose choke for pheasant, grouse, and decoying ducks. Neither extreme is "better" in isolation; a pattern that's too tight for the range costs you misses at close quarters, and one that's too open leaves gaps a bird can fly straight through.

Matching Choke to the Job

Field and clays disciplines lean on different ends of this same spread-versus-density curve. Skeet shooters favor Improved Cylinder or Skeet chokes because targets break close and fast, where a tight pattern actually costs hits. Trap shooters, engaging longer, going-away targets, move toward Modified or Full. Turkey hunters go further still, often stepping up to an aftermarket extra-full or turkey-specific choke paired with TSS or heavy lead to push effective, ethical range past what a standard Full choke delivers. As a rough field guide, Improved Cylinder suits inside 25 yards, Modified covers roughly 25-35 yards, and Improved Modified through Full extend useful range to 35-45 yards — always confirmed on paper, not assumed from the choke's stamped name.

How It Works

This calculator applies a simplified linear pattern-spread model long used by shooting instructors and choke manufacturers to estimate how a given choke behaves at a given distance, so you can compare tubes before you ever load a shell.

The basic rule:

  • Spread — Spread (inches) ≈ Distance (yards) × Choke Factor — Each constriction carries an approximate opening factor — roughly 1.0 in/yd for Cylinder, 0.85 for Improved Cylinder, 0.70 for Modified, 0.60 for Improved Modified, and 0.50 for Full. A tighter choke produces a proportionally smaller-diameter pattern at any given range, while density (the share of pellets landing inside a standard 30" circle) rises accordingly — from roughly 40% for Cylinder up to 70%+ for Full at the classic 40-yard reference distance.

These figures are planning estimates drawn from generalized choke behavior, not a substitute for shooting your own gun. Barrel-to-barrel and load-to-load variation is real, so pattern your actual shotgun, choke tube, and shell combination on paper at your intended hunting or competition range before you rely on it in the field.

Tips & Considerations

  • Match choke to range, not habit — a Full choke that killed turkeys at 40 yards will blow apart a rabbit flushed at 10.
  • Pattern your actual shotgun, choke tube, and shell on paper before hunting season or a match; two guns with the same choke stamp can pattern differently with the same load.
  • Larger shot sizes, like turkey loads with fewer, bigger pellets, need tighter constriction to hold density at distance than fine dove or skeet loads with high pellet counts.
  • If a pattern looks lopsided or shows a gap on paper, check for a loose choke tube, barrel damage, or a mismatch between choke and shot type (steel shot patterns differently than lead) before blaming the ammunition.
  • Extended-range and turkey-specific chokes (0.050"+ constriction) fall outside standard Full and should be patterned individually — the Full setting here reflects a standard factory Full, not an aftermarket extra-full tube.

Frequently Asked Questions

What choke should I use for turkey hunting?

Most turkey hunters run a Full or aftermarket extra-full/turkey choke paired with heavy lead or TSS loads to keep pattern density high enough at 30-40 yards to reliably put multiple pellets in the head and neck. A standard Full choke with premium turkey loads typically holds a usable pattern out to about 40 yards; extra-full tubes stretch that further but vary more between manufacturers, so pattern them individually rather than trusting the label.

Why does my pattern look eccentric or patchy instead of even?

An eccentric pattern usually traces back to the choke, not the ammunition — a dented or out-of-round barrel or choke, a loose screw-in tube, or a mismatch between choke constriction and shot type (steel patterns differently than lead through the same choke) are the most common causes. Rotate the choke tube 90° and reshoot: if the gap rotates with it, the tube is the problem; if the gap stays put, look at the barrel.

Does shot size change which choke I should pick?

Yes. Fewer, larger pellets — turkey loads in size 4-6 shot, or buckshot — need tighter constriction to hold density at distance simply because there are fewer pellets to begin with. Smaller shot with high pellet counts, like skeet loads in 8-9 shot, patterns well even through open chokes because there's enough volume to keep the circle full.

How is an aftermarket screw-in choke different from a factory one?

Factory fixed chokes and standard screw-in tubes generally track published constriction ranges — roughly 0.000" for Cylinder up to 0.035-0.040" for Full — but aftermarket tubes, especially long, back-bored, or ported designs, can pattern noticeably tighter or looser than the stamped name suggests. Always pattern an aftermarket choke on its own rather than assuming it behaves like a generic Modified or Full.

What's the effective range for each choke?

As a rough guide: Cylinder and Improved Cylinder are most useful inside 25 yards, Modified covers roughly 25-35 yards, and Improved Modified through Full extend effective range to 35-45 yards with the right load. Choke isn't the whole story, though — shot size, payload, and your own ability to judge distance and hold a pattern on target all matter as much as the tube in the barrel.

Is a tighter choke always better?

No. A pattern that's too tight for the range you're shooting reduces your margin for error and, at very close range, can produce an unbroken "doughnut" of shot that skirts around a bird instead of through it. Match the constriction to the range you actually expect to shoot, not the tightest tube in your case.
