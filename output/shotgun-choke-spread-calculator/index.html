Shotgun Choke Pattern Calculator

Estimate shot pattern spread by choke and distance

What Is the Shotgun Choke Pattern Calculator?

A shotshell doesn't fire a single projectile — a 12-gauge load of #6 shot throws roughly 225 pellets, and the instant they leave the muzzle they begin fanning out into a widening cone. Choke is the constriction built into or screwed onto the last few inches of the barrel that controls how tightly that cone holds together downrange. This calculator takes the choke you select — Cylinder, Skeet, Improved Cylinder, Modified, Improved Modified, Full, or Extra Full — along with your shooting distance in yards, and works out the two numbers every wingshooter and clay shooter actually needs: pattern spread (how wide the pellet cloud has opened) and pattern density (roughly what share of the charge is still inside that circle). A Full choke that throws a baseball-sized cluster at 20 yards holds a usable, dense pattern clear out to 40; the same shell through a Cylinder bore is already losing density by the time it reaches the bird.

Why This Calculation Matters

Choke selection trades forgiveness for knockdown power, and getting it wrong shows up as missed birds or crippled, unrecovered game — not as a line item on a spreadsheet. Pattern too tight for the range and you're throwing a 10-inch core of shot at a crossing dove: plenty of pellets, almost none of them touching the bird. Pattern too open and a 35-yard rooster slips through gaps between pellets too sparse to put multiple strikes into the vital area. Most wingshooters treat 3–5 pellet strikes as the threshold for a clean kill on upland birds, and hitting that threshold is a direct function of pellet count, shot size, and how much of the charge is still inside an effective circle at the range you're actually shooting. Working through choke-and-distance combinations here before you ever load a shell tells you whether that combination is putting enough pellets on target.

When Should You Use This Calculator?

Reach for this before matching a choke tube to a hunting trip or a round of clays — patterning a new gun, deciding between Improved Cylinder and Modified for opening-day quail over pointing dogs, checking whether a turkey gun's Extra Full tube still puts a killing pattern on a gobbler's head at 35 yards, or working out how far steel shot can realistically be pushed on decoying mallards. It's just as useful at the bench: run several distances side by side to see exactly where a choke's pattern opens past the point of reliable multiple strikes, instead of finding the limit on a wounded bird in the field.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Select Your Choke Tube: Choose the constriction you're patterning or planning to hunt with — Cylinder, Skeet, Improved Cylinder, Modified, Improved Modified, Full, or Extra Full. Most modern choke tubes are laser-etched with notches or a name on the base; an unmarked tube can be checked with a choke gauge or slugged to confirm its constriction.
  2. Enter Your Shooting Distance: Enter the realistic range in yards — not your longest possible shot, but where most of your shots actually happen. Upland birds typically break 15–30 yards, decoying waterfowl 20–35, pass-shot ducks 35–50, and turkeys 15–40.
  3. Click Calculate: The calculator applies that choke's spread rate and density curve to your distance instantly, entirely in your browser, so you can cycle through several chokes or ranges in seconds while deciding what to screw into the barrel.
  4. Read Spread and Density Together: Spread tells you how wide the pellet cloud is; density tells you how much of your charge is actually inside that circle. A wide pattern with low density means gaps a bird can fly through untouched — look for the choke that keeps density reasonably high at your real range, not simply the tightest pattern you can produce.

How It Works

Pattern spread isn't a single universal number — it depends on choke constriction, barrel length, shot hardness, wad design, and the individual gun. What follows is the patternboard-tested approximation that decades of shooters and choke makers have converged on: each constriction has a characteristic rate at which pattern diameter grows per yard of travel, and a characteristic share of the payload that stays inside a 30-inch circle at the traditional 40-yard test distance.

The basic rule:

  • Pattern spread rate — Spread (in) ≈ Distance (yd) × Choke Spread Factor — Cylinder bore opens at roughly 1.0 in/yd, Improved Cylinder around 0.75 in/yd, Modified near 0.60 in/yd, Improved Modified about 0.50 in/yd, and Full down near 0.45 in/yd. That's why a Full-choked pattern that's a tight 18 inches at 40 yards would sprawl to 35–40 inches through an open bore at the same range.
  • Pattern density (30-inch standard) — Density ≈ Pellets inside 30-in circle ÷ Total pellets in the shell — Measured at 40 yards on a patterning board, Skeet chokes typically run 45% density, Improved Cylinder about 50%, Modified around 60%, Improved Modified near 65%, Full about 70%, and turkey-specific Extra Full tubes 75–80% or more with the right load.

These are patternboard averages, not a guarantee for your specific gun. Choke dimensions vary a few thousandths of an inch between makers, and shell construction — plated versus unplated shot, shot-cup wads, buffering compound — can shift real density 5–10 percentage points either way. Shoot your own gun and load on paper before you stake a hunt or a tournament round on a number from any calculator.

Tips & Considerations

  • Pattern your actual hunting or competition load on paper at your real-world distance before trusting any number here — shot hardness, buffering, and wad design shift real patterns by several inches and several points of density.
  • For wingshooting, aim for a choke that keeps 3–5 pellets of your chosen shot size landing inside the vital area at your typical range, not the absolute tightest pattern you can produce — over-choking costs more misses than it prevents.
  • Steel and other non-toxic shot pattern tighter than lead through the same choke because it doesn't deform as it swages down through the constriction; many waterfowlers drop one choke size (Modified instead of Full) when switching from lead to steel.
  • Shot size and choke work together: smaller pellets like #7½ or #8 for skeet fill a pattern more evenly than large pellets like #4, #5, or BB for turkey loads, so the same tube can look noticeably sparser with a buffered turkey load than with field loads.
  • When two choke-and-distance combinations show similar density, favor the more open choke — a wider, evenly filled pattern forgives a slightly off mount or swing, while an over-tight pattern punishes any small aiming error.

Frequently Asked Questions

What choke should I use for what type of shooting?

Skeet or Improved Cylinder for fast, close clay targets and upland birds flushed inside 25 yards; Modified for general dove, pheasant, and sporting clays out to about 35 yards; Improved Modified or Full for pass-shooting ducks and longer clay presentations; Extra Full, almost always paired with a dedicated turkey load, for putting a dense pattern on a gobbler's head and neck at 30–40 yards.

What's the effective range for a given choke?

Effective range is where density stays high enough for several pellets to reliably land in the vital zone — roughly 20–25 yards for Skeet/Cylinder, 25–35 for Improved Cylinder, 30–40 for Modified, and 35–45+ for Full and Extra Full with a matched load. Past those distances, density falls fast enough that misses and wounded, unrecovered birds climb even when a handful of pellets still reach the target.

Why does the same choke pattern differently with different shot sizes or gauges?

Pellet count changes dramatically with shot size: a 12-gauge 1 oz load carries roughly 410 pellets of #8 shot but only about 135 pellets of #4 — more, smaller pellets fill a pattern more evenly and leave fewer gaps at the fringe. Sub-gauges (20, 28, .410) can also open a pattern slightly compared to a 12 through the same nominal choke, since their longer, narrower shot columns put more pellets in contact with the bore wall and increase deformation during setback.

Is this calculator a substitute for patterning my own shotgun?

No — use it for planning, not certainty. Individual barrels, choke tube manufacturing tolerances, and shell construction can shift a real pattern several inches wider or tighter than the patternboard averages behind this tool. Before opening day or a tournament, pattern your actual load on paper at your actual expected range and count strikes in a bird- or target-sized vital zone.

How does distance affect pattern spread and density?

Diameter grows roughly linearly with range — a Modified choke printing about an 18-inch pattern at 25 yards will be close to double that, around 36 inches, by 50 yards. Density falls faster than that, because the same pellet count is spreading across an area that grows with the square of the diameter. That's why a pattern that looks generous at 20 yards can turn dangerously thin by 45, and it's the main reason choke selection should match your real shooting distance, not your longest possible shot.
