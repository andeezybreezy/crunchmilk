Heat Index Calculator

Calculate heat index and heat danger level

Built for the Job Site, Not Just the Beach

Most heat index tools are built for someone deciding whether to go for a run or take the kids to the pool. This one is built for the briefing before the shift — for crew leads, safety coordinators, and outdoor workers who need to know, before boots hit the ground, whether today's combination of temperature and humidity crosses the threshold where heat-illness-prevention guidance calls for mandatory water breaks, shortened work cycles, or pausing non-essential outdoor tasks. Enter the ambient air temperature and relative humidity from a job-site thermometer or the nearest weather station, and you get the same feels-like value used in official heat advisories, plus the NWS danger band it falls into. Treat the number as a planning input for the crew, not a comfort rating for one person standing still in the shade.

A Worked Example: 95°F and 60% Humidity

Take a realistic late-summer job-site reading: 95°F air temperature, 60% relative humidity. Run those through the Rothfusz regression — the nine-term polynomial the National Weather Service built by fitting a walking-adult thermoregulation model to grids of temperature and humidity — and the heat index comes out to roughly 113°F. That's an 18-degree gap between what the thermometer says and what the body actually experiences, and it lands the reading in the NWS Danger band (103-124°F), a full category above the Extreme Caution you might assume from a 95°F reading alone. At that level, heat cramps and heat exhaustion are likely with continued exposure, and heatstroke becomes a real risk for anyone at moderate-to-heavy exertion without adequate water and shade. Now hold the temperature at 95°F but drop the humidity to 30%: the heat index falls to roughly 94°F — still Extreme Caution, but a materially different risk picture than 113°F. Relative humidity is doing more work in that swing than most people assume, which is exactly why a thermometer reading alone under-informs the decision.

From a Number to a Decision

Workplace heat-safety guidance increasingly scales specific actions to heat category rather than offering a vague reminder to stay hydrated: more frequent water and shade breaks once conditions cross into Extreme Caution, closer supervision and mandatory rest cycles in the Danger band, and rescheduling non-essential outdoor work in Extreme Danger. This calculator gives you the number those thresholds are built around — it doesn't replace a site-specific heat-illness prevention plan, a wet-bulb globe temperature reading where one is required, or the judgment of a supervisor who knows the crew's acclimatization and hydration status. Heat illness can escalate quickly and unevenly between individuals, so treat any reading in the Danger band or higher as a signal to intervene early, and treat symptoms like confusion, hot or dry skin, or loss of consciousness as a medical emergency, not something to wait out.

  2. Enter the Relative Humidity (%): Pull this from the same source as your temperature reading. On humid days this number does more to raise the feels-like value than the temperature itself.
  3. Click Calculate Heat Index: The calculator runs the full Rothfusz regression instantly and returns the feels-like temperature and its NWS danger band.
  4. Match the Result to Your Heat-Safety Plan: Compare the danger band against your organization's work-rest schedule or hydration policy, and adjust break frequency, shade access, or workload before symptoms show up.

How It Works

The NWS Rothfusz regression is a nine-term polynomial, not a simple lookup table — it was built by regressing Steadman's biometeorological heat-stress model against a grid of temperature and humidity combinations for an adult walking in the shade. It's the same equation federal and state occupational heat-safety guidance points to whenever it references "heat index," which is why this calculator runs the full polynomial rather than a simplified chart or a linear approximation between reference points.

The basic rule:

  • Heat Index (NWS Rothfusz Regression) — HI = -42.379 + 2.04901523T + 10.14333127R - 0.22475541TR - 0.00683783T² - 0.05481717R² + 0.00122874T²R + 0.00085282TR² - 0.00000199T²R² — T is dry-bulb air temperature in °F and R is relative humidity as a whole-number percentage. Below 13% RH with T between 80-112°F, a low-humidity correction term is subtracted; above 85% RH with T between 80-87°F, a high-humidity correction term is added. Below 80°F, the NWS instead uses a simpler averaging formula, since humidity has little effect on perceived temperature in that range.

Keep in mind the regression models a person walking in the shade at a light pace — it doesn't account for radiant heat coming off asphalt, metal roofing, or reflective concrete, and it doesn't account for moderate-to-heavy physical exertion or PPE that traps body heat and blocks sweat evaporation. Roofers, road crews, and framers routinely face effective heat loads well above what the calculator shows once you add a hard hat, a dark shirt, direct sun, and hours of sustained work — treat this output as your starting point for the day, not the ceiling for how hot the job will feel by early afternoon.

Tips & Considerations

  • Take the temperature and humidity reading from where the work is actually happening, not the nearest airport station — a rooftop or an asphalt lot can run several degrees hotter and drier than the official forecast.
  • Recalculate at midday and again in early afternoon; humidity and temperature don't move together, and the heat index can shift a full danger band even when the thermometer barely moves.
  • New or returning workers haven't acclimatized and reach heat strain at lower heat index values than a crew that's been working outdoors all summer — build in more frequent breaks for the first one to two weeks.
  • If the crew is wearing PPE, dark clothing, or working on reflective surfaces, treat the calculated heat index as a floor, not an accurate read of what they're actually experiencing.
  • Once a reading lands in the Danger band or higher, don't wait for a worker to report symptoms — build mandatory water and shade breaks into the schedule before the shift starts.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is heat index different from wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) for outdoor work?

Heat index is calculated from air temperature and relative humidity for a person in the shade with light wind. WBGT — the measurement many occupational heat-safety standards actually require — also factors in direct sun, wind speed, and radiant heat from surfaces like asphalt or metal roofing, measured with a physical sensor on site. Heat index is a useful planning estimate you can calculate from a forecast; WBGT is a more complete, on-site measurement. If your workplace heat-safety program specifies WBGT thresholds, use this calculator for early planning, not as a substitute for the required WBGT reading.

What work-rest schedule should I use at a given heat index?

NIOSH-style and many state heat-illness-prevention standards scale rest breaks and water intake to heat category, roughly: routine precautions below Caution, water and shade breaks worked into every hour in Extreme Caution, 15-plus minutes of rest per hour with mandatory water in Danger, and pausing non-essential outdoor work in Extreme Danger. Exact ratios vary by jurisdiction and workload intensity, so check your employer's written heat-illness prevention plan or applicable state standard for the specific numbers that apply to your job.

Does PPE change how the heat index applies to a worker?

Yes, significantly. The heat index formula assumes light clothing and unobstructed sweat evaporation. Impermeable suits, heavy turnout gear, or layered clothing trap heat and block evaporative cooling, so a worker in full PPE can experience heat strain at a calculated heat index well below the Danger band. Some heat-safety programs add a fixed number of degrees to the calculated heat index, or drop a full risk category, when workers are in enclosed or heavy protective gear.

How often should I recheck the heat index during a shift?

Recheck at the start of the shift and again at each planned break, since temperature and especially humidity can shift a reading from one danger band to the next over a few hours. Afternoon humidity often drops as temperature peaks, which can leave the heat index roughly flat even as the thermometer climbs — the only way to know is to recheck both numbers, not just the temperature.

What heat index level should trigger stopping outdoor work?

There's no single number that applies everywhere — it depends on jurisdiction, industry, and the specific workload — but a heat index in the Extreme Danger range (125°F and above) is treated as a stop-work trigger for non-essential outdoor tasks in most heat-illness prevention plans, and the Danger band (103-124°F) is typically where mandatory rest and closer supervision begin. Follow your employer's written plan or the applicable state standard, since several states set numeric heat index thresholds directly into their heat-illness prevention regulations.

Is a calculated heat index the same for every worker on a crew?

The number itself is identical for everyone standing in the same spot, but individual heat tolerance varies widely with hydration, sleep, medication, age, body size, and acclimatization to outdoor work. Two workers under the same 105°F heat index can have very different risk levels — treat the calculated number as the baseline for the group and watch individuals for early symptoms regardless of where the reading falls.
